On slow software

By Ada Byron

Most software is built to be fast, and most of the time that is the right goal. A text editor that lags behind your typing is a broken editor. But there is a second kind of speed that we rarely question: the speed at which a tool pushes you forward, finishes your sentence, and tells you what to think next.

Writing is slow on purpose. The pauses are where the thinking happens, and a tool that fills every pause with a suggestion takes away the part of writing that matters most. I have started to prefer tools that wait for me, that keep my notes where I left them and stay quiet until I ask.

What waiting looks like

Waiting software has a few habits in common. It keeps everything in plain files, so nothing is locked away. It remembers where you were. It lets you link one idea to another without asking you to file anything first.

  • It never interrupts a sentence.
  • It shows its work when it does help.
  • It can be ignored entirely.

The best tool for thinking is the one that gets out of the way.

const pause = () => think();

None of this is new. Paper notebooks have worked this way for centuries, and the best digital tools borrow their manners from them rather than from the attention economy that shaped most of the software we use every day.
