# Kintsugi and gold

The first time I saw a kintsugi bowl I thought the gold was decoration. It is not. The gold is a decision: this break is part of the object now, and we are not going to pretend otherwise.

> The bowl is more valuable because it can show you what happened to it.

That sentence belongs in Repair is a kind of attention, or maybe in On craft and repair. I am not sure yet which essay it is for. The clip Golden joinery has the history; the note has the feeling.

My grandmother kept a cracked teapot on the top shelf for thirty years. Nobody was allowed to use it, and nobody was allowed to throw it out. I used to think that was sentimental. Now I think it was the most honest object in the house, because it was the only one that admitted it had been dropped.

Most of what we own is designed to hide its history. A phone case gets swapped when it scuffs. A table gets refinished. We sand the evidence away and call it care, and I think that is a kind of lying we have all agreed not to notice.

A workshop rule I want to keep: make the repair visible, make it strong, do not make it loud.

The interesting thing about the rule is the order. Visible comes first. Strength is the second thing you check, and only after that do you worry about whether it looks good. Most of my writing goes the other way round: I polish a sentence long before I know whether it can carry any weight.

Maybe that is the actual essay. Not bowls at all, but drafts. Every draft I keep has a few places where it broke and I patched it, and the patches are usually the parts people quote back to me.

I tried an experiment last spring. I left every revision in a document, struck through, instead of deleting it. Reading it back a month later was strange and useful, like reading the notes of someone who had the same thoughts as me in a different order.

The rest of this draft is still in the notebook. Why we sleep is not relevant, but it is the tab I keep leaving open.
